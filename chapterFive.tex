\chapter{Qualitative Analysis and Results} \label{chapter: qual analysis&results}
This chapter begins with the reasoning for using Reflexive Thematic Analysis to analyze the open-text responses of the personal data and perceived control items in Section \ref{sec: Reflexive TA}, the analytic process that was followed in its implementation can be found in Section \ref{sec:qual analysis}. Four themes were constructed from the responses, each discussed below with its sub-themes.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Theme 1: What makes `Data' personal? \ref{T1:personal data} 
    \item Theme 2: Deficits in privacy agency and awareness \ref{T2: Lack of agency} 
    \item Theme 3: Corporate opacity and accountability gaps \ref{T3:Corporate scam} 
    \item Theme 4: Structural Governance shortcomings \ref{T4:regulation issues} 
\end{itemize}


As discussed in Section \ref{Subsec: control}, the participants could elaborate on their choices regarding perceived control over personal data processing through optional open-text fields. These were included to capture the nuances arising from this sensitive yet necessary discourse. Post data collection, approximately 100+ responses per item were obtained, with considerable variation in response length and depth. Additionally, the open-text question regarding the Personal Data definition in Section \ref{Subsec: IUIPC} also yielded diverse answers covering various interpretations of what constitutes personal data. Upon initial familiarization, many responses demonstrated substantive variations in their perceptions across a range of discourses in this field. Additionally, some users expressed contradictory viewpoints to those of the majority, making them crucial in showing lack of blank agreement throughout the study. This richness coupled with the exploratory nature of the study, informed the decision to employ inductive thematic analysis \cite{braun_using_2006} for deeper interpretation and theme construction. This way we center participants' perceptions and present them using data-driven qualitative themes instead of pre-defined concepts.

\section{Reflexive Thematic Analysis} \label{sec: Reflexive TA}

Given the researcher's interpretive role throughout the research process in terms of assumptions, from shaping the survey design to theme development during analysis, Reflexive Thematic Analysis aligned with the epistemological commitment to foreground subjectivity as an analytic resource rather than treating it as bias \cite{braun_using_2006, braun_reflecting_2019}. Thematic Analysis offers the methodological flexibility that requires the researcher to iteratively think and understand what they're working with, therefore helping their own perspective change and evolve with each iteration. This flexibility combined with the structured six-stage analytic process provides a comprehensive way to understand the patterns within the data and construct themes from them. These six stages are: 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Familiarizing yourself with your data
    \item Coding
    \item Generating initial themes
    \item Developing and reviewing themes
    \item Refining, defining and naming themes
    \item Writing up
\end{enumerate}

Other methods like qualitative content analysis \cite{hsieh2005three} or codebook thematic analysis  offered methodological rigor similar to reflexive TA. However, they do not fully account for the positionality of the researcher and how it impacts the analysis. In contrast, Reflexive TA preserves such nuances while also ensuring that the responses of the participants are not forced into pre-defined categories. 

Analysis was conducted by a single researcher, consistent with reflexive TA, which positions the analyst’s interpretive engagement as central to theme generation rather than as a source of error to be controlled for through multiple coders. Inter-rater reliability was therefore not applicable, as it presumes a single correct coding of the data \cite{mcdonald2019reliability, braun2021one}. My positionality as a Non-Resident Indian, who resided in the country till the completion of my Bachelor's degree influenced the way I initially understood the context and narrative around the conversation of personal data privacy from a Global South perspective, where the norm for privacy is often from a shared perspective \cite{sambasivan_privacy_nodate}. This reflexive thought process was employed during all the phases of conducting this research and is visible in the theme development as well. However, any assumptions made implicitly were documented through memos to maintain provenance \cite{braun2021one}.     

\section{Analytic Process} \label{sec:qual analysis}

In order to stay true to Braun and Clarke's approach \cite{braun_using_2006}, the responses for these six control items and the personal data definition were read through twice in a thorough manner to obtain \textbf{familiarity with the data}. This consisted of $\sim$ 800 open-text responses, annotating information that was caught on first glance. Using the institutional instance of Atlas.ti \cite{atlasti}, these responses were then \textbf{coded together as a singular group}, since it was observed that they had many recurring narratives that repeated across items. This process resulted in the generation of 72 codes on the first pass. These codes, along with the reasoning for assigning some of them in a certain manner was noted for accuracy purposes. Additionally, they were also discussed with my supervisors, who have between them, a strong expertise in qualitative methodologies \cite{braun2021one}. Feedback was provided about some codes having generic scope or being worded too vaguely. These codes were then revisited individually and either re-worded or even recoded from scratch where necessary. Subsequently, more descriptive code names were sought, to ease the process of grouping them at a later stage. This process ensured that the rigor was maintained throughout the coding phase, without losing the nuance mentioned by the participants. This second pass of coding resulted in the generation of 119 codes. 

This set of codes was then sorted in Miro \cite{miro}, a collaborative platform for having a broader overview of all the existing information, and to help sort them better. In the process to \textbf{generate suitable themes}, the codes were gathered and sorted together based on their relevancy to the plausible themes. The themes were then \textbf{reviewed} against the initial codes and their respective quotations, helping to triangulate this entire process iteratively. Each of the identified themes and their subsequent sub-themes were then explicitly \textbf{defined and named} in terms of their scope. These themes and sub-themes were then extensively discussed with my supervisors to ensure clarity and soundness in their alignment. Finally, the entire process was cross-checked, along with the selection of certain relevant quotes for each code and documenting the choices that were made to ensure a sound analytic procedure. 

Two LLM-generated responses were earmarked under the code ``LLM response'' and omitted from any form of thematic analysis, to ensure the perspectives were kept human and their quality maintained. Similarly, responses which were in plain binary agreement or disagreement across all the questions were marked separately but left out of theme building since they do not contribute in a meaningful way. Responses (n=8) which were repeated by the same users across multiple questions, were only considered once while the rest were marked as duplicate and omitted. Finally, responses like `No reason', `Same as before', `NA' and repetition of the question statement were also marked and omitted since they do not contribute to the quality of the analysis in a meaningful manner. 

Since the personal data question required the users to name some items which they want to assign into this category, this list of items mentioned across each response was marked as a bucket code. This was done to ensure the nuance present in the rest of the response was not lost and the codebook was not filled up with just names of the various personal data items. Since this bucket code was present for every response of the personal data question, it is considered as a supportive pillar while creating the theme around these codes.


Upon segregation, there are the 4 themes which have been identified from user responses, with each having sub-themes to further explain the various nuanced perspectives. The consolidated list of all the themes and their sub-themes along with a brief explanation and example quotations is as follows:

\section{Theme 1: What makes `Data' personal?} \label{T1:personal data}
This theme focuses on the user definition of their personal data, how they use and create such data. Additionally, it also deals with the importance of their consent related to data usage or access by others.

\subsection{Identity focused information} \label{T1.1:Identity}
Importance of individual identity and its intertwined nature with personal relations. Users describe identification to be both direct and indirect in its nature, while such personal data is perceived to be always in need of privacy and security or already having it

        \begin{quote}
            \textit{``Any data that represents my identity or belong to me''}
        \end{quote}
        \begin{quote}
            \textit{``Any personally identifiable information, including health information that can trace me or my family's identity''}
        \end{quote}
        
\subsection{User-originated attributes} \label{T1.2:Attributes}
Explains the users opinions on how data is just something that they generate, therefore it describes them in a unique manner. Due to such uniqueness, some users also claim that the companies use this data to build a digital profile of their identity for tracking or advertising purposes
        \begin{quote}
            \textit{``Anything which gives away any key unique trait/identification/interests of mine''}
        \end{quote}
        \begin{quote}
            \textit{``Any data generated by me that can be used to influence my behaviour''}
        \end{quote}
        
\subsection{Consent, Ownership, and Selective Access}  \label{T1.3:Access of info}
Focuses on the importance of users' consent, their perspectives about not sharing such data with anyone and selective access. Some users also talk about their willingness to share such data with others, with the caveat of their choice still being present nonetheless
        \begin{quote}
            \textit{``Its a confidental data which shouldn't be visible to others without the owner consent.''}
        \end{quote}
        \begin{quote}
            \textit{``Anything that belongs to me is personal. Until I deliberately choose it to be public.''}
        \end{quote}
        \begin{quote}
            \textit{``My personal data means related to my details so before using it need to take permission of me''}
        \end{quote}

    
\section{Theme 2: Deficits in privacy agency and awareness} \label{T2: Lack of agency}
This theme discusses user pitfalls with respect to consent revocation and data deletion practices. Their lack of awareness about such practices while not having sufficient information being provided to them by the companies was explored. This leads to the users showcasing mixed feelings of overall trust, primarily leaning towards corporate distrust. 

\subsection{Agency constraints and powerlessness} \label{T2.1:users powerless}
Captures the various fears of the users in terms of their social standing against the companies stemming from confusion, data misuse, presence of AI, and lack of choice and control. While some users echo their right to have control over data, a few also contradict this by talking about their existing control and how this benefits them due to the personalized content. Many users also recollect their personal experiences of being digitally-profiled and subsequently being spammed with promotional un-consented advertising. They also claimed that such organizational data malpractices often also lead to possible cybercrimes.
        \begin{quote}
            \textit{``Generally I get recommendations and I don't know how they know it''}
        \end{quote}
        \begin{quote}
             \textit{``I didn't want to become victim of cyber crime , abuse because of my opinions , don't want them to track my movements, calls me on birthday and asking for donations , morphing photos , getting loans my using my details.''}
        \end{quote}
        
\subsection{Procedural knowledge inadequacies} \label{T2.2:Not transparent process}
Describes the plight of users about their awareness and knowledge gaps regarding the consent revocation, data deletion, and data processing practices. Many reported never thinking about these processes until prompted, while others knew these rights existed but lacked clarity on how to exercise them. A minority do mention that they find consent revocation specifically quick and easy, especially due to automated tools which facilitate this action.
        \begin{quote}
            \textit{``If there are multiple stakeholders who own/share access to the data, the removal process is complex and could have mistakes''}
        \end{quote}
        \begin{quote}
            \textit{``Not that at all easy, first not much awareness that one can withdraw and also not easy to find the procedure to withdraw''}
        \end{quote}
        \begin{quote}
            \textit{``In the past, I have requested my personal data to be deleted with my account when I no longer use the service (ex: food delivery apps, job application sites). The process gas been quick and simple.''}
        \end{quote}
        
\subsection{Post-hoc confirmation challenges} \label{T2.3:Noconfirmation}
Deals with users talking about not receiving any valid and believable confirmation primarily related to their consent withdrawal and deletion of their data. Claims of an illusion of deletion being created by the companies are made mainly because the data will always exist on the cloud and their servers
        \begin{quote}
            \textit{``There are features to whitdraw but I don’t know if they actually do it or not''}
        \end{quote}
        \begin{quote}    
            \textit{``Because I am not sure that if they comply and remove my data, what if it has already been stored in third party servers and cannot be erased from there.''}
        \end{quote}
        
\subsection{Contingent trust} \label{T2.4:Mixed trust}
Focuses on the conditionality of users' trust in data deletion and consent revocation processes based on the type of company implementing the process or the legislation they are required to comply with. More importantly, trust as a conditional factor is judged against the companies based on their size, location and inherently assumed motivation. Some users explicitly mention about making decisions on their own, which often leads to them trusting the companies and their processes.
        \begin{quote}
            \textit{``While some websites/applications support withdrawing there are many they don’t support or they do it a complicated way which make withdrawing impossible.''}
        \end{quote}
        \begin{quote}
            \textit{``I do not trust them to delete anything. I've asked companies to stop sending me fucking ads and they still do''}
        \end{quote}
        \begin{quote}
            \textit{``I believe many reputable websites and applications provide accessible privacy settings or support channels that make it relatively easy to withdraw consent or request data deletion. Clear instructions and user-friendly processes help users exercise their privacy rights.''}
        \end{quote}

    
\section{Theme 3: Corporate opacity and accountability gaps} \label{T3:Corporate scam}
This theme describes the various beliefs held by the users regarding the companies' deceptive practices and their legal non-compliance to generate profits. Furthermore, users also talk about companies' influence and monetary-based practices to ensure sustenance of such non-compliance.

\subsection{Notice without substance} \label{T3.1:Mixed info}
Describes participants' accounts of disclosure that is given but not sufficient. Participants reported being notified about data processing, consent withdrawal, and data deletion, yet described the information as being insufficient and in some cases absent altogether. Others reported that consent was not sought at all, with vague user agreements used as the basis for processing. 
        \begin{quote}
            \textit{``I am often informed that my data is being processed through privacy notices or consent pop-ups, but the information is frequently lengthy, technical, or vague. As a result, I am not always sure what data is actually being collected, how it is used, or whether I have meaningful control over it.''}
        \end{quote}
        \begin{quote}
            \textit{``As there is nothing specific mentioned anywhere in the website to withdraw the personal details provided. ''}
        \end{quote}
        \begin{quote}
            \textit{``Many sites don seek consent sharing sensitive details and take liberty under vague user agreement''}
        \end{quote}
        
\subsection{Manipulative design} \label{T3.2:Design messup}
Dark patterns like Roach Motel, Trick wording, Nagging, among others, are mentioned by the users as practices these companies use with the intent to misguide, often resulting in the users skipping the documentation. These dark patterns are briefly explained in Section \ref{subsec:dark patterns}.
        \begin{quote}
            \textit{``Signup is often one click but try deleteing any account and count your number of clicks''}
        \end{quote}
        \begin{quote}
           \textit{``Companies usually make it super hard to withdraw consent by hiding/fine printing info as to how to go about it so users lose interest''}
        \end{quote}
        \begin{quote}
            \textit{``Annoying cookies pop up -> reject it -> you get another annoying pop up. People would might as well accept it and get on with it but no body knows what information is being collected at that point of time.''}
        \end{quote}

        
\subsection{Corporate evasion and greed} \label{T3.3:Moneypriorities}
Users discuss the ways companies evade legal compliance, stemming from their lack of accountability and their monetary end-goals. They extensively discuss pseudo-legal compliance citing real-world examples like Aadhaar.
        \begin{quote}
            \textit{``It is not beneficial for companies,to alert the customer,as usually people don't trust multi national conglomerates, so they do legally so but in a way,that people don't immediately grasp the importance and just give in, without going through all that information...I hold the notion of personal marketting to lowest esteem, because it makes me feel constantly perceived,and gives an air of declining privacy of mine''}
        \end{quote}
        \begin{quote}
            \textit{``Aadhar details of 800million people got leaked. Who really gives a fuck in India. There's no recourse for private leaks as well''}
        \end{quote}
        \begin{quote}
            \textit{``Although many companies claim to follow data protection laws, I am not always confident that they fully comply with them in practice. News reports about data breaches, privacy concerns, and the misuse of personal information by large technology companies have made me more cautious. While some organizations likely take these laws seriously, I believe others may focus more on appearing compliant than on consistently protecting users’ personal data.''}
        \end{quote}


\section{Theme 4: Structural Governance shortcomings} \label{T4:regulation issues}
This theme explores the various hiccups in the legal processes for the users ranging from lack of awareness, procedural hassles not being worth it and, primarily, enforcement gaps. Users also hope for a change and improvement in these shortcomings.

\subsection{Epistemic legal uncertainty} \label{T4.1: mixed legal awareness}
Deals with conditional awareness, knowledge, and confidence of the users about the various laws. Even the users who claim to have legal knowledge mention their skepticism towards the effectiveness of such laws. However, a few users show belief in the existing legal systems, instead expressing doubts about the companies and their compliance.
        \begin{quote}
            \textit{``I don't think there's that much awareness Laws don't get implemented so easily''}
        \end{quote}
        \begin{quote}
            \textit{``There are obviously laws but I dont know the effectiveness since laws vary from country to country and an individual cant know laws of all the various countries and it often do happens you data is sold not only within your country but also out of the country to various data brokers.''}
        \end{quote}
        
\subsection{Enforcement barriers} \label{T4.2:enforcement gaps}
Conditionality is further extended towards the distrust in the enforcement procedures either due to organizational noncompliance or lack of transparency. Users believe that the hassles involved in fighting against these giant corporations only amplifies their distrust on both the companies and the legal procedure itself.
        \begin{quote}
            \textit{``I believe many organizations strive to comply with data protection laws, but the level of compliance can vary depending on the organization's size, industry, and commitment to privacy. Without transparency or independent verification, it is difficult to be certain.''}
        \end{quote}
        \begin{quote}
            \textit{``I believe legislation still needs to catch up to the developments and practices of tech companies. Also, legal recourse for an average member of the public can be prohibitively expensive in a country where justice is not always delivered in a timely manner.''}
        \end{quote}

\subsection{Wishful reforms} \label{T4.3:futurehopes}
Users acknowledge the need for better legal procedures, increased awareness and improvement in the overall legal implementation.
        \begin{quote}
            \textit{``Enforcement in a defined timeline is a must which I feel we are lacking''}
        \end{quote}
        \begin{quote}
            \textit{``Even if there are, people are not aware of such laws. People should be educated in such laws''}
        \end{quote}


The overlap between these themes, especially how despite high sensitivity perceptions, corporate opacity contributes to agency deficits within the context of regulatory permissiveness, is explored as interconnected pathways in Section \ref{chapter:discussion}, alongside the quantitative results to address the research questions \ref{rq:1} and \ref{rq:2} in a broader analytical manner.
