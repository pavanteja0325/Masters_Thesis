\chapter{Quantitative Analysis and Results} \label{chapter: Quant Analysis&Results}
This chapter first provides information about the analyses of the quantitative data and their subsequent results, beginning with the examination of the adapted IUIPC scale in Section \ref{sec: IUIPC analysis}, followed by the various clusters of personal data items in Section \ref{sec: PD analysis}, and the perceived control of the users of their data processing in Section \ref{sec: Perceived control analysis}. Finally, the possible effects of various demographics on all of these analyses are examined in Section \ref{subsub:demo effects}. Additionally, the methods used to analyze the questions related to DPDPA have been discussed in Section \ref{sec:DPDPA analysis}.


As seen in chapter \ref{Sec:Privacy Notice}, the survey primarily consists of Likert-based questions, which were in the form of 5-point (Sensitivity scale and Agreement scale) or 7-point (Agreement scale) scales depending on the section in which they were used. Participant responses to these questions were initially checked for completeness and then coded to each scale's numeric equivalent values of 1-5 or 1-7 to ensure ease in calculations \sa{ensure ease in calculations isn't a good reason, a citation for this practice is more logical}. Likert responses are considered to be ordinal in nature, due to the presence of ordered data but without any clear intervals between them which meant that non-parametric tests were to be used. Descriptive statistics including the median, variance and skew were computed for each section, along with frequency distributions. The median was used as the measure of central tendency given the ordinal nature of the responses, while variance and skew indicated the spread of agreement among participants and the direction in which responses clustered. The mean was reported only to indicate the rough response split and was not used for any further comparison. Frequency distributions were reported, since descriptive statistics alone can obscure polarized or bimodal response patterns. 

\section{IUIPC Subscales} \label{sec: IUIPC analysis}
The Internet Users' Information Privacy Concern scale was adapted (See \ref{Adapted_Survey}) to best capture the perspectives of the Indian users as recommended by Malhotra et al. \cite{malhotra_internet_2004}. As noted in Section \ref{Subsec: IUIPC}, changes like rewording the statements were made in order to ensure that the scale retained its legibility despite the change in context. A high ceiling effect was found in the responses which can be seen in the frequency distribution table (See figure \ref{fig:IUIPC Frequency}).

Since Pearson's correlation \cite{pearson1895note} assumes normality and only looks for linear relationships, Spearman's correlations \cite{spearman1904} were used given the ordinal nature of the responses. As the IUIPC was rephrased, item groupings could be assumed to hold; correlations of each item with items from its own and other sub-groups were therefore examined to verify that items grouped as intended. The Collection and Control items correlated well within their own sub-scales and weakly with the others. This convergent-discriminant pattern indicates that the two sub-scales captured distinct and internally coherent constructs, supporting their use as separate sub-scales. In contrast, the Awareness items correlated weakly with each other, plausibly because some participants misread the reverse-keyed statement. As these participants passed the attention check and provided sufficient open-text responses, inattentive responding was ruled out. These item to item correlations can be seen in the Figure \ref{fig:IUIPC correlations}.

Internal consistency of each sub-scale was assessed using Cronbach's $\alpha$ \cite{cronbach1951coefficient}. As $\alpha$ is considered as a single aggregate estimate, it indicates neither the precision of such estimate nor which values weaken it. So, 95\% confidence intervals were computed using Feldt's method \cite{feldt1987statistical} alongside corrected item-total correlations (r.drop) and alpha-if-item-dropped values \cite{nunnally1994}, which show how strongly each item relates to the remainder of the sub-scales and whether its removal would improve consistency respectively. All reliability statistics were computed using the \texttt{alpha()} function in the \texttt{psych} package in R \cite{revelle2026psych}.

The Awareness sub-scale returned an $\alpha$ of 0.22 with wide Feldt confidence intervals at 0.04 - 0.37. Additionally, the r.drop value for Item 6 in this sub-scale was 0.0024, and removing it raised the sub-scale's $\alpha$ to 0.777. These values confirmed that the item was not measuring the same construct as the others. However, dropping it would mean that the Awareness sub-scale would only contain two items which is not recommended \cite{eisinga2013reliability}. The Awareness sub-scale was therefore excluded from further analysis. The Control and Collection sub-scales had $\alpha$ values of 0.76 and 0.77 respectively and were retained for subsequent cross-analysis. 
Alpha statistics for all the three sub-scales are available in the Appendix. \ref{control_IUIPC} \ref{collection_IUIPC} \ref{awareness_IUIPC}

A Friedman rank-sum test \cite{friedman1937use} was conducted to assess differences in sensitivity ratings across the 23 personal data items. The test indicated significant differences in the perceived privacy sensitivity amongst the different data items, with $\chi^2(22) = 2564.3$, p < .001. Kendall's W \cite{kendall1939problem} \footnote{Kendall's W measures the effect size by normalizing the value of Friedman's between 0 (no agreement) to 1 (complete agreement)} revealed moderate inter-rater agreement at W = 0.45. Together, this meant that the participants did not treat all items as equally sensitive and broadly agreed on which items were more sensitive, although with considerable individual variation in the ordering. This is also in alignment with the frequency distribution seen in Figure \ref{fig:Frequency sensitivity}.

To examine the relationships between sensitivities of these groups against the aggregated values of IUIPC's Collection and Control alongside the \textit{perceived control} value of each participant \sa{This is from the last section of the survey right? worth mentioning and internally referring it since this is the first time you're using it for analysis}, Spearman's rank order corelation \cite{spearman1904} was calculated. 27 correlations were formed across the 3 aggregate values (IUIPC's Control, IUIPC's Collection and perceived control) and 9 personal data groups (demographic, financial, identity, technical, contact, photographs, fingerprints, passwords, medical) which were denoted by Spearman's $\rho$. To control for Type I error inflation from multiple hypothesis testing, p-values were adjusted using the Holm-Bonferroni sequential correction method, which is more powerful than traditional Bonferroni adjustment while maintaining family-wise error rate control. Statistical significance was evaluated at $\alpha$ = 0.05 following Holm-adjustment.

\subsubsection{Correlation with IUIPC Collection}
All nine data sensitivity categories showed statistically significant positive associations with IUIPC Collection concerns (all p\_holm < 0.05), ranging from small effects (passwords: $\rho$ = 0.142) to medium effects (technical data: $\rho$ = 0.321; identity documents: $\rho$ = 0.307).

\subsubsection{Correlation with IUIPC Control}

Similarly, eight of nine categories showed significant positive correlations with IUIPC Control, with the notable exception of demographic data ($\rho$ = 0.077, p\_holm = 0.215). The strongest association was observed for identity documents ($\rho$ = 0.373), followed by medical records ($\rho$ = 0.305).

\subsubsection{Correlation with Perceived control}

In contrast to the other two constructs, \textit{Perceived control} demonstrated predominantly negative (inverse) relationships with data sensitivity. Two categories showed significant negative correlations: identity ($\rho$ = -0.203, p\_holm = 0.009) and medical ($\rho$ = -0.194, p\_holm = 0.014). Seven of nine associations were non-significant, with correlations near zero (For example, technical: $\rho$ = -0.019; demographic: $\rho$ = -0.023). 

However, the ceiling effect seen in the IUIPC subscales \ref{fig:IUIPC Frequency} and in several personal data items including \textit{Medical records and history}, \textit{Passport details}, \textit{Passwords}, and \textit{Aadhaar details} \ref{fig:Frequency sensitivity} is a major caveat to be remembered while understanding these values. Coincidentally, the group of personal data items which has any notable variance across participants is \textit{demographics}, which either showcased no significant correlation or only a small effect size ($\rho$ < 0.184) across the three constructs. \section{Control over processing} \label{sec: Perceived control analysis}
Responses to the six items in this section were distributed similarly across items as seen in Table \ref{tab:perceived_control_descriptive} and Figure \ref{fig:Perceived_control_distribution}. To determine whether these items could be combined into a single aggregate score, Spearman's rank-order correlation \cite{spearman1904} was calculated to understand the monotonic relationships between them. The items showed a moderate to strong correlation (0.36 - 0.70) with one another as shown in Figure \ref{fig:Percevied_control_correlation}. Internal consistency was then assessed using Cronbach's $\alpha$ \cite{cronbach1951coefficient}, which returned a high $\alpha$ value of 0.86 and 95\% Feldt Confidence Interval of 0.84 - 0.89 \cite{feldt1987statistical}. This meant the six items can be combined into a single aggregate value for each respondent. This aggregate value is termed as the \textit{Perceived Control} of the users, as the items collectively understand the users' perceptions of how much control they believe to be having over the processing of their personal data and over the laws intended to help them in such situations.

In order to better understand how the user valued the general control over their personal data and their perceived control of its processing practices, IUIPC's control sub-scale scores of every respondent were aggregated and compared against their \textit{Perceived Control} values. Spearman rank-order correlation was conducted to examine this relationship. A weak, statistically significant negative correlation was found, $\rho$ = -0.18, p = .004, indicating that as IUIPC control increases, perceived control tended to decreases. However, it is to be noted that the previously observed pronounced ceiling effect in IUIPC control plays a significant role in ensuring this result cannot be considered at face-value due to the lack of variance.

For this reason only one of the two was considered while looking at their effects, as effect isolation would not be possible due to the very high overlap\footnote{While primary Regression analysis only considers Gender and Field of work as the indicators, Age was also considered as an indicator and the subsequent effects were calculated to ensure robustness. It was seen that there were only 64\% of the men were having technical backgrounds and the lower overlap meant that the effects could be looked at both as bivariates and individual indicators while isolating one another. \sa{Unclear sentence, try to rephrase and make it more readable e.g., lower overlap with what?}

To check if \textit{Gender} and \textit{Field of work} demographics of the respondents showed any effect on the variance of personal data sensitivity, Mann Whitney U tests were conducted comparing women versus men and technical versus non-technical groups across the 22 categories. To account for Type I error inflation from multiple hypothesis testing (Since the same data had to be run across 23 tests), p\_values were adjusted using the Holm-Bonferroni sequential correction method. This method was chosen as it is stricter than traditional Bonferroni adjustment while maintaining family-wise error rate control. Additionally, effect sizes were also calculated using r = Z/$\sqrt{N}$ %(Cohen,88) 
to understand how much impact the significant values are having on each item. As shown in Figure \ref{fig:Sensitivity MWU}, both \textit{Gender} and \textit{Field of work} yielded no statistically significant effects after Holm adjustment. Ordinal logistic regression models were also estimated to understand the isolated effects of the demographics, however, none of their coefficients achieved statistical significance after Holm correction (all p\_holm > 0.12).

Similarly, the effects of \textit{Gender} and \textit{Field of work} on the variance of \textit{perceived control} value for each respondent were measured using Mann Whitney U tests. Like with the previous comparison, both the demographics yielded no statistically significant effects with p = 0.117 and p = 0.296 respectively as seen in Figure \ref{fig:Perceived control MWU}. Linear regression model was estimated to understand the isolated effects on this aggregate value, however, none of them achieved statistical significance (p > 0.12). Both the regression plots are provided in the Appendix for reference purposes.

As an exploratory test, the perceptions of Non-Resident Indians were compared against those currently residing in India, to observe if residency status played any role in their sensitivity and perceived control perceptions. NRIs accounted for 15\% (n=39) of the total sampled population (Table \ref{tab:privacy_statistics}). 

It is important to note that these results cannot be used for any plausible explanations even within this sampled population, due to the small NRI demographic (N=39) and unaccounted factors such as migration history, personal beliefs, and differing regional regulations.

Within this exploratory framework, the NRI sub-sample showed statistical significance for 
\textit{Medical records and history} (p\_holm = 0.026, r = 0.20) and \textit{Passport details} (p\_holm = 0.014, r = 0.21) on sensitivity measures (Figure \ref{fig:Sensitivity MWU}), though with less-than-medium effect sizes. Additionally, Figure \ref{fig:Perceived control MWU} shows that the domestic respondents expressed higher perceived control than NRIs, with a s statistically significant difference of a small effect size (p = 0.012, r = 0.16). Potential directions for future work building on this discourse are discussed in Section \ref{sec: future work}.

Responses were initially analyzed in a simple descriptive manner by aggregating the number of participants who answered each question as shown in Table \ref{tab:dpdpa-awareness}. For the open-text of the respective questions, data is presented in a simple descriptive summarization to ensure the codes stayed as close to the original responses as possible. More than half of them (52\%) additionally state that they are either not sure or do not think that the implementation of this Act would help in safeguarding their personal data data privacy. Sentiments of partial effectiveness (n=10), distrust in enforcement procedures (n=7), lack of trust in government (n=6) were echoed in detail as part of the open-text responses, mirroring emotions displayed in the rest of the survey.
