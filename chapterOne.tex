\chapter{Introduction}

Data breaches have grown exponentially, with over 1800 reported breaches in the first half of 2026, putting it on course to set a new annual record. AI has accounted for an increase of over 56\% of breaches from the previous year and with over 6 billion Internet users as of April,2026, placing the need for personal data governance at an all-time high. While newer regulations are constantly being adapted to ensure compliance, that hasn't hindered malicious actors from playing their hand and affecting millions worldwide. This need for personal data privacy is further emphasized when large-scale infrastructural breaches such as the 2026 Canvas data breach occur, compromising 275 million users and their Personally Identifiable Information (PII) worldwide. While laws such as the General Data Protection Act (GDPR) in the E.U, the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA) in the USA and the Personal Information Protection Law (PIPL) in China have reinforced user data protection, enabling stricter regulations and tighter controls worldwide.

With a population of over 1.47 billion people, online penetration of 70\% as of October, 2025 and a new Digital Personal Data Protection Act (DPDPA) in partial enforcement from November, 2025, India is heading towards being a landmark example of data privacy enforcement in de-centralized privacy environments. However, this also provides a certain opportunity for the possibility of large-scale breaches, especially with an existing troubled past in this regard. This new act which was first passed in 2023, aims to reinforce India's digital landscape, focusing on the user's autonomy over their digital personal data, while providing lawful practices for organizations to process such data. Prior works on the Act discuss about the user's understanding of its laws and the need for such regulations. However, past research in aforementioned established contexts has shown that when such regulations are implemented without the consideration of the general population's needs and understanding, it could lead to frustration over certain practices, loopholes for organizations to bypass and overall skepticism on the implementation. 

Considering that the DPDPA is heading towards complete enforcement and compliance from all the organizations from November, 2026, it is therefore important that the users' perspectives on their personal data privacy be understood to get a holistic perspective of the actual needs that need to be fulfilled and protected by the Act. While many legal experts have dissected the Act's definitions, discussing their vagueness and subsequent implications, an understanding on the possible gap between the user's perceptions of such definitions and processes to the actual provisions by the Act would establish a baseline comparative differentiation to rely upon. 

To address this, an exploratory online survey with 259 primarily educated, English-speaking participants with potential familiarity of online spaces was conducted. The analysis focused on the end user's perspectives of their general privacy perceptions of their personal data, their perceived sensitivity of the various personal data items and perceived control over the processing of such data. The survey addresses the following research questions:

    \RQ What are Indian Internet users’ perceptions about the privacy-sensitive nature of different types of personal data? \label{rq:1} \\
    \RQ What are Indian Internet users' beliefs regarding their control over different types of personal data and its processing? \label{rq:2}

The analysis revealed that the users in this sample clustered the sensitivity of their personal data items based on the type of data, primarily rating \textit{financial, identity and medical data to be extremely sensitive whereas their demographic data was the most polarizing in terms of variance}. This disproves the Act's notion of considering the processing of all personal data in a similar manner, and re-emphasizing the need to focus on individual data sensitivities. Additionally, the respondents expressed \textit{mixed levels of perceived control} over the processing of their data, predominantly stating \textit{distrust over organizational implementation and compliance evasive practices}. User distrust was also visible in their perceptions on the weak regulatory landscape of the country, emphasizing on the need for better accountability against the companies. Notably, these results are not influenced by their demographics, meaning that the observed variances in sensitivity and perceived control stands true throughout this sample. 

Overall, these emotions of high privacy sensitivity coupled with middling levels of control over processing practices due to external failures explore the dimensions of digital resignation amongst this sampled population. Coupled with the loose regulative measures, this highlights the compliance gaps present in this environment and the fulcrum of this problem lying in the heart of such a multi-dimensional issue. Given the country's scale, its reliance on social brain for privacy decisions, it is therefore essential to further understand the lay-person's perspectives on how such policies can be implemented and subsequently help the broader population in the matters of safeguarding their personal data.
