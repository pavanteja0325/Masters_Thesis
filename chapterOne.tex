\chapter{Introduction}

Data breaches have grown sharply in recent years, with over 1800 breaches reported in the first half of 2026 alone, putting the year on course to set a new annual record. AI-enabled attacks account for a reported 56\% rise over the previous year. With more than 6 billion Internet users worldwide as of April 2026, the volume of personal data in circulation, and the number of people affected when it leaks, is larger than at any previous point. 

Meanwhile, the tech industry is competing to win the \textit{AI race} using smart glasses, personal AI agents, or smartwatches, which further aggravates risks of non-consensual data collection, processing, and surveillance for the users. Large-scale infrastructural breaches further increase these stakes. The 2026 Canvas data breach alone compromised the Personally Identifiable Information (PII) of 275 million users worldwide. These incidents prompt the need for regulations that ensure users' autonomy over their personal data and its processing remain intact. Laws such as the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in the EU, the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA) in the USA and the Personal Information Protection Law (PIPL) in China reinforced these measures to a certain extent, especially during the days of their initial implementation. However, their efficiency in dealing with ambiguous definitions and weak enforcement has come under scrutiny, as past works have shown that lay-users struggle to understand, adopt and exercise the provisions of these laws to their benefit.

\section{Research Motivation}

India presents an unusually consequential scenario. With a population of over 1.47 billion people, online penetration of 70\% as of October 2025, and a new Digital Personal Data Protection Act (DPDPA) in partial enforcement from November 2025, the country is positioned to become a landmark example of data privacy enforcement in a de-centralized privacy environment. This same scale widens exposure, in a country with a troubled history of large-scale data breaches.

The Act, first passed in 2023, aims to reinforce India's digital landscape, focusing on the user's autonomy over their digital personal data, while providing lawful practices for organizations to process such data. As the DPDPA moves towards full compliance from November 2026, the question of whether its provisions match what the users actually want to protect becomes rather pressing. 

Research in more established regulatory contexts like the EU and the USA suggests why this matters. When regulations are implemented without accounting for the needs and understanding of the general population, the result is often user frustration and broad skepticism towards enforcement.

\section{Research Gap}

Legal experts have dissected the DPDPA's definitions at length, noting their vagueness and the implications that follow. Prior work has also examined the users' interactions with cookie banners, which the Act mandates, and their general privacy perceptions when using web applications. These works also briefly examine the general awareness and perceptions about some of the Act's clauses. What remains largely unexamined is the primary focus of the Act: user privacy of their personal data and their control over the processing practices possible on this data. 

Two provisions of the Act make this gap particularly salient. First, the Act treats personal data as a single undifferentiated category, applying uniform obligations regardless of data type. However, prior large-scale studies conducted in 2006 and 2012 found that Indian users rated their financial data as highly sensitive. It is therefore necessary to understand if the current legislation matches the perspectives of the present population whose opinions on sensitivity might have changed with time. Secondly, the Act grants users rights over processing, including access, erasure and consent withdrawal, on the assumption that these rights translate into meaningful control. Whether users perceive that control, and what shapes that perception, has not been established. 

\section{Research Questions}
This study addresses the above-mentioned gaps by answering the following research questions:

    \RQ What are Indian Internet users’ perceptions about the privacy-sensitive nature of different types of personal data? \label{rq:1} \\
    \RQ What are Indian Internet users' beliefs regarding their control over different types of personal data and its processing? \label{rq:2}

\section{Contributions}

To answer these questions, an exploratory online survey with 259 primarily educated, English-speaking participants with likely familiarity with online spaces was conducted. The survey combined Likert-style and open-text questions across three sections: general privacy perceptions, privacy-sensitivity of personal data items, and perceived control over processing. This was followed by three questions on users' DPDPA awareness and privacy beliefs. This work makes the following contributions:

\textbf{Addressing \ref{rq:1}}, the analysis shows that the users in this sample clustered data sensitivity by data type rather than treating it uniformly. \textit{Financial, Identity and Medical data were rated extremely sensitive with near-zero disagreement, whereas demographic data was the most polarizing}. In this sample, the Act's notion of considering processing of all personal data items in a similar manner is challenged, re-emphasizing the need to focus on item-level and contextual data sensitivities. 

\textbf{Addressing \ref{rq:2}}, respondents reported \textit{slightly below-average levels of perceived control with wide variation across the sample}. While there was a partial agreement on the existence of laws which can aid them, users predominantly stated that they do not trust the companies to actually comply with such regulations. \textit{Lack of post-hoc confirmation and usage of dark patterns by the organizations} in addition to the weak regulatory landscape of the country further eroded trust, leaving it contingent rather than earned. Users also called for stronger accountability mechanisms to keep the companies in check. Notably, no significant differences were observed across demographic groups, suggesting that these patterns hold broadly within this sample. 

Taken together, high privacy sensitivity alongside limited perceived control attributed to external failures explores the dimension of digital resignation within this sampled population. Coupled with the loose regulative measures, this highlights the compliance and enforcement gaps present in this environment. Given India's scale and the role of social influence in privacy decision-making, understanding the lay user's perspective is essential to designing policies that can genuinely help the broader population safeguard their personal data.
