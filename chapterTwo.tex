\subsection{Digital Personal Data Protection Act} \label{subsec:DPDPA explanation}

Digital Personal Data Protection Act \footnote{This Act is denoted as DPDPA or the Act where appropriate.} is an Indian legislation related to users' personal data privacy and its processing. It was introduced on 11th August, 2023. The Act provides individuals with the rights to protect their personal data and only allows organizations to process such data for lawful purposes. \textit{Personal Data} in this regard is explicitly defined as `any data about an individual who is identifiable by or in relation to such data', replacing the previously used term of Sensitive Personal Information (SPI) in the Information Technology Rules, 2011. 

The Act is applicable when processing of digital personal data within the territory of India or outside Indian territory if the processing is related to users residing in India. While the user is required to provide their explicit consent for such processing, any data which is otherwise made public by the user themselves does not fall under its jurisdiction. This ambiguity coupled with a singular definition for what constitutes personal data goes against the notion of users considering some data types to be more sensitive than others \cite{danezis_privacy_2006, kumaraguru_privacy_2012}. 

Additionally, the Act defines the term \textit{processing} of personal data to include any operation or set of operations performed on such data including collection, recording, organisation, structuring, storage, adaptation, retrieval, use, alignment or combination, indexing, sharing, disclosure by transmission, dissemination, restriction, erasure or destruction. The user is given the right to be provided with detailed information of what data is being processed and its purposes in an explicit manner. They are also given the provision to withdraw the provided consent for processing at a later stage, and request the deletion of their data. These requests must be adhered to by the companies to the extent possible under legal provisions, and this process must explicitly be as easy and straightforward as the process of providing consent. 

\subsection{Dark Patterns} \label{subsec:dark patterns}

The following dark patterns are coined by UX designer Harry Brignull \cite{brignull-leiser-2026} \cite{brignull-old}. These were mentioned by the respondents and are discussed in the Section \ref{T3.2:Design messup}.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Roach Motel}: The user finds it easy to sign up or subscribe, but when they want to cancel they find it very hard
    \item \textbf{Trick wording}: The user is misled into taking an action, due to the presentation of confusing or misleading language
    \item \textbf{Preselection}: The user is presented with a default option that has already been selected for them, in order to influence their decision-making
    \item \textbf{Obstruction}: The user is faced with barriers or hurdles, making it hard for them to complete their task or access information
    \item \textbf{Privacy Zuckering}: The act of creating deliberately confusing jargon and user-interfaces which trick users into sharing more info about themselves than they really want to
    \item \textbf{Hidden costs}: The user is enticed with a low advertised price. After investing time and effort, they discover unexpected fees and charges when they reach the checkout
    \item \textbf{Nagging}: The user tries to do something, but they are persistently interrupted by requests to do something else that may not be in their best interests
\end{enumerate}

\section{Relevant Work} \label{sec:literature}
Privacy perceptions of users are often shaped by their awareness, knowledge and existent legislation to ensure enforcement. These interrelated notions are explored in Section \ref{subsec:regulatory impact}. Previous literature has found that cultures with more communal aspects play a significant role in shaping human perceptions \cite{altman1977privacy}. To understand this impact of culture, the notion of privacy in cross-cultural contexts is examined in Section \ref{subsec:cross culture}. One such context where culture and its intricacies shape the way users perceive privacy is that of India. This contextual nuance is important, given the timeline of DPDPA's enforcement and its plausible impact on user privacy of their personal data. It is discussed further in Section \ref{subsec:indian context}.  

\subsection{Regulatory Impact on Privacy Perceptions} \label{subsec:regulatory impact}
The term \textit{Privacy} by definition can be categorized in a broad manner, without ever covering its actual meaning related to an individual. This nuance is only exemplified when the context of one's environment has a lasting impact in the way they perceive their privacy \cite{nissenbaum2004privacy}. Personal data privacy therefore, should ideally incite the strongest reactions of protection and safeguarding, since it involves dealing with information which is closest and dearest to the user with respect to their everyday lives. However, past works \cite{zou_ive_nodate, mayer_now_nodate, karunakaran_data_nodate} in established contexts show us that while the users display mixed awareness about the breach of their personal data privacy, more often than not they believed that such breach would not impact them in any reasonable manner. This situation reflects the 'Privacy Paradox' \cite{acquisti_economics_2016}, where people claim concern for their privacy yet take few measures to safeguard it. Effectively, this means that the burden of privacy is being transferred to the user. However, users might also take such decisions due to lack of user control cultivated by organizational malpractices i.e., Digital Resignation \cite{draper_corporate_2019} or exhaustion from repeated violations of their privacy i.e., Privacy Fatigue \cite{choi2018role}. This exhaustion often leads to user disengagement and futility about their privacy practices i.e., Privacy Cynicism \cite{hoffmann_privacy_2016}.  
 
Regulations such as GDPR and CCPA have been introduced to help the users by providing the necessary safeguards to take control over the provision, processing, and usage of their personal data. Despite initial skepticism towards the effectiveness of the individual claims and rights by the public \cite{strycharz_data_2020}, users were hopeful in the implementation of safeguards and maintained that it would eventually lead to more privacy-focused infrastructural development \cite{mangini_empirical_2020}. Although the intention was to help the masses have an understanding of their control and rights, it has not translated into actual privacy practices. This left the users lacking an understanding of the privacy disclosures due to the ambiguity on why their data is being collected \cite{chen_fighting_2021}, alternative ways for organizations to obscure their data practices \cite{oconnor_clear_2021}, disclosure of irrelevant information and overwhelming the users when they explicitly want practices to be transparent and fair \cite{kyi_it_2024}. 

In summary, despite the efforts of such regulations to safeguard user privacy and reduce their burden, users feel left behind, often having to find their own workarounds. The current work explores these notions of users' perceived control in a newly enforced regulatory context, to better understand such after-effects of the shift in the privacy burden. 

\subsection{Cross-Cultural Differences} \label{subsec:cross culture}
Schomakers et al. \cite{schomakers_internet_2019} compared the privacy perceptions of German users against those from the Brazilian and the US contexts, to understand if there were any differences in their perspectives on the sensitivities of the different data items. They observed some distinct differences in sensitivities although the rank ordering remained stable. On an individual level, they noted that disposition to value privacy, risk propensity, and education level influenced the perception of sensitivity within this German sample. Herbert et al. \cite{herbert_world_2023} conducted a large-scale longitudinal survey to understand the role of demographic factors on the misconceptions regarding various security and privacy practices. They noted that the country of residence played a key role in users withholding such beliefs, while also suggesting the possibility of Internet literacy and technological readiness having an influence. Using the same dataset, Herbert et al. \cite{herbert_digital_nodate} later tried to understand the differences in the digital security \& privacy perceptions and practices of participants from WEIRD versus non-WEIRD countries. They focused on user sensitivity of various data items, risks posed by attackers, previous experiences, among others. They found that participants from non-WEIRD countries scored higher across all measurement variables showcasing higher awareness, perception, and exposure in terms of their security and privacy practices. One of their key findings was that the non-WEIRD participants relied heavily on their friends and family for digital security and privacy advice, which showcases communal perspectives and social responsibilities towards these practices.

Examining similar notions but from a qualitative perspective, Umbach et al. \cite{umbach_your_2023} compared user perceptions of the security \& privacy practices of participants from Brazil, Philippines, India, Egypt, and Nigeria. One of their primary findings was that the participants highly valued their financial data and were wary of scammers in this regard, while also talking about their device-sharing methods obstructing their individual right to privacy. This behavior was also noted across other Global South contexts with Ahmed et al. \cite{ahmed_everyone_2019, ahmed_digital_2017} finding that while economic need was a driving factor in device-sharing, it also stems from deep-rooted social and cultural practices in Bangladeshi societies. Furthermore, they found that existing power dynamics and patriarchal practices were also amplified due to these privacy practices, thereby revealing the impact on women's privacy autonomy in these societies. Such power structures in the context of personal data privacy were even more prevalent in the Arab Gulf, where Abokhodair et al. \cite{abokhodair_privacy_2016, abokhodair_photo_2017} observed that decision making on social media and photo-sharing platforms involved not just the users' concerns of their safety and privacy but also an adherence to their religious and societal beliefs. Naveed et al. \cite{naveed_ask_2022} conducted interviews with Pakistani users from low-literacy, low-income backgrounds regarding their privacy choices and its implications on their personal data. Users echoed similar sentiments of religious and societal beliefs of patriarchy playing a key role in especially impacting the women's management of privacy choices and control over their 'personal data'. In a more centralized and governed Internet environment, Zhou et al. \cite{zhou_understanding_2024} found that at least half of their sampled Chinese users believed in their government's rights to access their personal data. Although some of them were reluctant in sharing their information, they felt powerless in the broader societal context. 

To summarize, users across various contexts expressed varying sensitivities towards the different types of personal data, rating them based on their daily lives which are heavily influenced by societal and cultural contexts they are situated in. Similarly, privacy perceptions differed based on collective device sharing models, familial and societal norms, often blurring the lines of individualistic control which forms the basis for privacy legislation. The current work examines the possibilities of such variances in privacy sensitivities of users towards their personal data, while navigating the societal and cultural landscape situated against the DPDPA enforcement in India. 

\subsection{Indian Context in the Wake of DPDPA} \label{subsec:indian context}
Kumaraguru et al. \cite{kumaraguru_privacy_2012} conducted an empirical study on the user perceptions of their privacy choices with a sample of 10,427 participants across India, and found that the participants were split in their opinions on what constituted personal data while being more privacy-sensitive about their data. They also observed that the participants were considering financial data to be one of the most important forms of personal information, due to their awareness of various financial scams. However, this work was conducted in 2012, limiting its applicability today, given the growth of Internet penetration, user awareness, and judicial changes since that time period. More recently, works in this context focused on low-income users and their navigational challenges while using the identity infrastructures \cite{srinivasan2018poverty} and adapting their privacy safeguards to deal with the novelty of the Internet \cite{popuri_i_nodate}. Both these works find that the users were apprehensive about the privacy impact of their personal data and viewed sharing it as a trade-off against what they gain in return. Additionally, there was a mismatch in their understanding of formal controls and their everyday practices, since they often relied on family members and other intermediaries to help safeguard their privacy as seen across other Global South contexts above. This reiterated the shared model of device usage and privacy perceptions, showcasing the depth of these roots. 

Pinch et al. \cite{pinch_someone_2022} also observed the drawbacks of this shared device model, when trying to understand the predicaments faced by queer men from semi-urban suburbs using geo-social networking applications. This issue also extends to the work of Murthy et al. where they understand the security \& privacy practices of the older family members from middle-income households which are comparatively rich in their technical affinity. They note that the families often allocate the responsibility of handling such social interactions to an individual, who more often than not, walks the fine line of supervision and paternalism to ensure protection \cite{murthy_individually_2021}. Kapania et al. note ineffective and often non-existent institutions as one of the primary reasons for users to be influenced in their perceptions of AI \cite{kapania_because_2022}. Users exhibited signs of authoritarianism which manifests into attitudes of self-blame, faith, forgiveness and gratitude towards such AI systems. They also find that, despite not having adequate understanding of such systems, users depicted blind belief in their choices and decisions since it \textit{might be better than what they currently have}. Overall, these works deal with the notions of lack of awareness and exhaustion towards the system, stemming from distrust towards the companies and conditional belief in the government, primarily regarding the beneficial returns received. 

DPDPA was introduced to deal with these notions and benefit the users in safeguarding their personal data privacy by giving back users their control. The Act focuses on personal data being a singular undifferentiated category in its treatment while previous works have observed variances in user rankings across personal data sensitivities. Priyadarsini et al. \cite{priyadarsini_web_2025}, who focused on users' web privacy perceptions, found that in their sample, the participants were skeptical about sharing specific personal data like financial information. They also noted that the users were more aware of sharing such information on social-media applications than on e-commerce platforms. Since this work was situated after the Act was officially passed, they also tried to understand users' awareness of it and found middling levels of response especially in the younger demographics. In a similar time frame, Athar et al. \cite{athar2026} examined user interactions with cookie banners and how they perceived some DPDPA laws. They observed that the users demonstrated high privacy concerns yet often chose options equating to lower privacy in their interaction with the cookie banners. They equated this to the notion of 'privacy paradox' being present within their responses along with an overall sentiment of distrust in the government's enforcement of the Act. Given the reported distrust, this may equally reflect the attitudes of digital resignation from the participants. However, these works were situated at a time when DPDPA was at its infancy and yet to be in enforcement. Moreover, they exclusively dealt with narrower aspects of user privacy and were conducted in situational contexts. 

Although this provides evidence of extensive research on user privacy perceptions in the Indian ecosystem, it still does not explain the perspectives of the users on their personal data and its processing which is the primary focus of this Act. To the best of our knowledge, this is among the first works trying to understand the privacy perceptions of Indian Internet users following the enforcement of DPDPA, primarily focusing on their personal data sensitivities and perceived control over its processing. This helps in understanding if there exists a mismatch in the user comprehension to what the Act caters, or if the Act actually resolves the needs of the users. Understanding this could help in minimizing the potential mismatch, thereby helping operationalize and enforce the Act in a better manner.
